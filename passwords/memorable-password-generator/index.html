Strong passwords you can actually remember

The most secure password is one that is long, random and unique to a single account. The problem is that random strings like x7#Qp!2vL9 are almost impossible to remember. A passphrase solves that: a few random words joined together, such as Lantern-Walrus-Cobalt-Meadow-27!, is long enough to be hard to crack but easy to picture and type.

This is the approach behind the UK National Cyber Security Centre's advice to use three random words. Our Memorable Password Generator follows the same idea and lets you choose how many words to use, whether to add a number and a symbol, and what kind of words to use.

Choose a theme

Pick a theme from the menu to change the words the generator uses. Everyday words uses a list of common objects and places. The other themes use words from football, pop, rock, 1980s culture, sci-fi films and TV, or darts. A theme you enjoy can make a passphrase easier to remember, but the words are still chosen at random, so the result is just as unpredictable.

The address in your browser updates when you change theme, so you can bookmark your favourite.

How many words should I use?

Each extra word multiplies the number of possible passphrases by the size of the word list. With the Everyday words list, four random words alone can be combined in about 190 billion ways, before the number and symbol are added. Six words take that into the tens of quadrillions.

Smaller themes, such as darts, have fewer words to choose from, so add one or two extra words to make up for it. As a guide:

  • 4 words for low-risk accounts, such as forums and newsletters.
  • 5 to 6 words for shopping, social media and streaming accounts.
  • 6 words or more for your email, bank and anything holding personal information, plus two-step verification wherever it is offered.

Keeping your passphrase safe

  • One password per account. Reusing a password means one leak can unlock several accounts.
  • Keep it random. Swapping a generated word for your pet's name or birth year makes it easier to guess.
  • Use a password manager to store a different passphrase for every site.
  • Change it after a breach. If a site you use reports a data leak, generate a new passphrase for it.

Private by design

Every passphrase is generated in your browser using its built-in cryptographic random number generator. Nothing you create is sent to our servers, stored or logged.

Frequently asked questions

Is a passphrase really as secure as a random password?

A long passphrase can be stronger than a short random password, because its length makes it harder to crack. What matters is that the words are chosen at random, not picked by you.

Why add a number and a symbol?

Many websites require them. They also add a little extra strength, but length does most of the work.

Can I use spaces instead of hyphens?

Some sites don't allow spaces in passwords, so we use hyphens. You can change them after copying if the site accepts spaces.
