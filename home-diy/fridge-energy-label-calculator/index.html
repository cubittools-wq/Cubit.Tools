What a fridge costs to run

A fridge or freezer is switched on every hour of the year, so its efficiency has more effect on your bill than almost any other appliance. The energy label gives the electricity it uses in a year, in kilowatt-hours. Multiply that by the price you pay per kilowatt-hour and you have the yearly running cost.

Running cost formula

Yearly cost = Annual energy use (kWh) x Price per kWh

Worked example

A fridge freezer uses 240 kWh a year. At 28p per kWh, it costs 240 x £0.28, which is £67.20 a year. A model that uses 150 kWh a year would cost £42, a saving of £25.20 every year for as long as you own it.

Energy classes A to G

Fridges and freezers sold in the UK carry a label with a class from A, the most efficient, to G, the least. The scale was reset in 2021, replacing the old A+, A++ and A+++ ratings, and the new classes are much stricter. An appliance that was A+++ under the old scheme may be a C or D today, so the two cannot be compared directly.

The calculator uses a typical yearly consumption for each class. The class boundaries depend on the size and type of appliance, so a large American-style fridge freezer uses more electricity than a small under-counter fridge of the same class. For an accurate figure, use the kWh per year printed on the label of the model you are looking at.

Is a more efficient fridge worth the extra?

Compare the difference in price with the difference in running cost over the life of the appliance. A fridge freezer commonly lasts ten years or more. A model that costs £80 more to buy but saves £25 a year pays back the difference in just over three years and saves money after that.

Ways to cut the running cost

  • Set the fridge to between 3 °C and 5 °C and the freezer to minus 18 °C. Colder than that wastes electricity.
  • Leave a gap behind the appliance so air can move over the coils.
  • Check that door seals close tightly, and let hot food cool before you put it in.
  • Defrost a freezer that is not frost-free when ice builds up.
