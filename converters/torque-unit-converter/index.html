What is torque?

Torque is a turning force. It is the force applied multiplied by its distance from the point of rotation, which is why a longer spanner makes a tight bolt easier to undo. A force of 10 newtons applied at the end of a 1 metre lever produces a torque of 10 newton metres.

Where torque figures are used

  • Tightening bolts. Wheel nuts, cylinder head bolts and bicycle components all have a specified torque. Too loose and they can work free, too tight and threads or parts can be damaged.
  • Engines and motors. Torque describes pulling power: how strongly an engine can turn the wheels at a given speed.
  • Power tools. Drill drivers and impact wrenches are rated by the torque they can deliver.

Worked example

A car handbook gives the wheel nut torque as 110 Nm, but your torque wrench is marked in pound-feet. Divide 110 by 1.3558 to get 81.1 lb-ft, and set the wrench to 81.

About the units

  • Newton metres: The metric unit of torque, written Nm. Used in workshop manuals and on torque wrenches.
  • Pound-feet: The imperial unit, written lb-ft or ft-lb. Used for engine torque in the UK and the United States.
  • Pound-inches: One twelfth of a pound-foot. Used for small fasteners.
  • Kilogram-force metres: An older metric unit, written kgf m, still found in some manuals.

Frequently asked questions

Is lb-ft the same as ft-lb?

For torque, yes. Both mean pounds of force multiplied by feet of distance. Strictly, foot-pound is also a unit of energy, which is why engineers prefer pound-foot for torque.

Should I always use a torque wrench?

For anything safety related, such as wheels, brakes and suspension, yes. Always use the figure in the manufacturer's manual.
